Marking assistant for teachers

Marking eats your evenings. Markless takes the first pass.

Point it at a class set — a Google Form export, a scanned stack from the photocopier, or photographs of individual papers — and get back question-by-question marks with half and quarter marks, a written justification for every deduction, and feedback short enough that a fourteen-year-old will read it. You override anything you disagree with. The app never has the last word on a grade.

Four ways to mark, and the fastest is the one nobody guesses

Times are for a class of thirty. Most teachers end up using two of these — one for quizzes, one for real tests.

~2 min

Google Form or spreadsheet

Quizzes, and anything students type. Nothing to scan at all.

Export the responses to CSV, confirm the columns it found, and tap the correct option for each multiple-choice question. Multiple choice is marked on your phone for free — only the written answers use credits, and every student's answer to one question is marked together, so the whole class is judged the same way.

~6 min

Prepare, print, then scan the stack

Real tests on paper. The fastest paper route, and the only one that cannot mix students up.

Markless builds one print-ready PDF containing every copy, each stamped in the footer of every page with its own code. Feed the collected stack through the copier's document feeder as one PDF, and pages sharing a code reassemble into one paper — shuffled, creased or out of order. Stamping runs entirely on the device.

~6 min

Scan a stack you already printed

A test that has already been written on, printed the ordinary way.

Scan the stack to one PDF and it splits back into papers, either by a fixed page length or by on-device detection of cover-page signals. Detection uploads nothing; the split is shown to you first, and only what you confirm is marked. Tell it how many students to expect and it catches a page the feeder swallowed.

~15 min

Photograph each paper

A handful of papers, a late submission, or anywhere without a copier.

Prop the phone up and slide each page underneath. It shoots automatically and tells you — with a sound or a buzz — when the page has landed and it is time to slide the next one.

The first paper is always marked on its own and shown to you before the other twenty-nine go ahead. A wrong answer key costs one paper, never thirty. This holds on every plan, including free — approving the first result is a safety check, not a premium feature.

Real marking, not a vibe check

Two rules shape the whole product, and they do not bend at the edges.

  • Question-by-question scores, with half and quarter marks, and a justification for every deduction.
  • Feedback in your voice, not a robot's — specific, kind, and short enough that a student will read it.
  • Answer keys. Scan the key once and every paper is marked against it, or let Markless learn the key from the first paper of a stack.
  • It flags what it should not decide alone. A hand-drawn diagram, an unreadable page, a right answer reached by an unexpected method — it says so and asks for you, instead of guessing a score.
  • Ontario KTCA categories, and curriculum expectations for your region.
  • Marks leave as a gradebook CSV, or as a Google Doc in your Drive.
  • Lesson planning — a quiz, worksheet or lesson plan drafted from a topic.
  • Your work follows your account. Sign in on a new phone and your classes, students and every marked test are there.

  • The teacher decides the grade. Always.

    Markless produces a proposed mark. Every result opens in a review screen showing the score, the per-question breakdown and the reasoning behind each deduction. You can change any mark, and an overridden result is flagged as such. Nothing is transmitted to a gradebook, a student or a parent by Markless — you carry the mark forward yourself.

  • Student identity does not go to the AI.

    The marking model grades the work. It is not told, and does not need to know, whose work it is. The name is read on the device and blacked out before the page is uploaded; imported Form answers go up keyed by row number, never by name.

  • Overnight costs about a fifth of on the spot.

    A class set can be submitted to a batch queue and collected later, which is roughly five times cheaper per paper. It is why every plan goes so much further that way.

What leaves the phone, stated plainly

You would be handing us other people's children's work. Here is what happens to it, including the parts that are not perfect.

  • The student's name stays on the device, on the iOS and Android apps. On-device text recognition finds identity fields — Name:, Student:, Student ID: — and those regions are painted solid black in the copy that is uploaded. The original, unaltered page stays on your phone, so you still see the real paper. The name is used locally to file the result under the right student.
  • Imported Form and CSV answers are sent keyed by row number, never by name. The name column is never transmitted, and known student names are scrubbed out of the answer text itself. Multiple-choice questions are marked entirely on the device and are never transmitted at all.
  • Page images are not retained. They are passed through for marking and discarded. What is stored is the result — scores, comments, question breakdowns — plus a one-way hash of each page, so re-marking an identical page is served from cache instead of being processed again. A hash cannot be reversed into the image.
  • No ads, no tracking, no analytics SDK, no device advertising identifier, no location, no contacts. There are no student accounts and no student-facing surface at all.
  • Delete your account from inside the app and the profile, every marked result, every class and student, answer keys and settings go with it, on the device and on the server. It is irreversible.

Redaction is controlled by Settings → Privacy → "Hide student names before marking", and is on by default on iOS and Android.

Where the promise stops

  • Redaction covers name fields. It cannot cover a name written somewhere unexpected, a name inside the body of an essay, or a signature on artwork.
  • If the handwriting is unreadable to the recognizer, the field is not found and the page uploads unredacted. The app reports when that happened.
  • The on-device recognition this depends on is a native library, and a browser has none. In the web version nothing is read off the page and nothing is blacked out: photographed work is sent exactly as it was picked, with whatever name is written on it. The setting is disabled there and says why, and the first browser upload asks the teacher to acknowledge it — but saying so is not the same as fixing it. For photographed work, use the app, or the Form and CSV route, which is unaffected.
  • Handwriting is itself arguably identifying, and nothing removes that.
  • One cheap route, used for objective and short answers, goes to a provider whose published terms permit training on submitted content and store data in the People's Republic of China. It receives answer text only — never images, never names — and it can be switched off, but it does not meet the no-training bar the rest of the pipeline holds to.
  • There is no SOC 2 report and no third-party security audit, and no signed DPA template yet — one is available on request.

Markless reduces exposure substantially. It does not make a scanned page anonymous, and no vendor should claim otherwise. The full write-up — subprocessors, legal footing, retention and every known gap — is in Security and Compliance.
